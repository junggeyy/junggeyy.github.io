the cost of1°

Everyday convenience we rarely question

By Vickey Ghimire September 26, 2026 · 6 min read

I speed-walk from the bus stop to my class, trying not to sweat through my blue shirt in the 101°F Dallas heat. After eight minutes of stride, I enter the building to be greeted by that first blast of air conditioning. Phew, the relief!

It’s glorious for the first few minutes, but as the heat fades, that cool relief starts taking a toll. I look around to find some of my classmates putting their sweatshirts on, sipping their iced lattes, and preparing to sit through the three-hour lecture in what now feels less like a classroom and more like a freezing vault. I fold my arms together to get cozy for the lengthy lecture, regretting, for about the fourth week in a row, that I didn’t bring my hoodie.

There is something genuinely absurd about the need—or even the thought—of grabbing your hoodie when your phone pings you every morning about the Excessive Heat Warning.

The same story applies to plenty of indoor spaces across the states. Commercial buildings, however, are complicated to judge, as the thermostat there has to account for hundreds of people, multiple heat-radiating devices, ventilation, and so on. But let’s narrow down to the spaces people do have personal control over: homes.

Air conditioning has become so embedded in everyday American life that it is easy to forget just how extraordinary a convenience it is. On those extreme days, cooling can be an absolute necessity, but the line between necessity and added comfort often begins to blur with every drop of a degree on your thermostat. And I think those extra degrees are fair to interrogate.

So, let’s explore what actually happens beyond the thermostat when we ask for just one more degree of cooling.

One degree, quantified

In 2020, air conditioning accounted for about 19%—254 billion kilowatt-hours (kWh)—of total electricity consumption in U.S. households, with 162.5 billion kWh consumed during the peak summer period from June through August [1]. Those 162.5 billion kWh, spread across 123.53 million households and 92 summer days, gives the equivalent of roughly 14.3 kWh of cooling electricity per household per day.

Show the baseline calculation
\[ \frac{162.5\text{ billion kWh}} {123.53\text{ million households}\times92\text{ days}} \approx 14.3\,\frac{\text{kWh}}{\text{household}\cdot\text{day}} \]

This is not a claim that every household’s air conditioner consumed 14.3 kWh every day. It is a baseline that translates a national quantity to household scale. Regional and household-level values can differ dramatically, and factors such as insulation, humidity, equipment efficiency, shade, occupancy, and weather all matter.

ENERGY STAR recommends setting your thermostat as high as comfortably possible during summer, typically around 78°F for maximum efficiency and savings [2]. Yet a Consumer Reports national survey in 2021 found that the average temperature American households set their thermostat to is approximately 71°F, with nighttime preferences going below 68°F for better sleep [3].

While there is no universal formula pointing that one degree of change equals a fixed percentage increase in electricity consumption, a commonly cited rule of thumb is that lowering the thermostat by one degree can increase cooling costs by roughly 3–5%.

For this illustration, let’s use 78°F as an energy-saving reference point and the midpoint, 4%, as an estimate of the change in cooling demand. If we take our baseline of 14.3 kWh per summer day, lowering the thermostat by one degree would represent:

\[ 14.3\,\text{kWh}\times\frac{4}{100}=0.572\,\text{kWh}\tag{i} \]

That is 0.572 additional kWh of electricity consumption per day in our simplified model, taking the baseline values.

Other than the increase in your electricity bill with this extra consumption, there is a much more significant hidden cost: the emissions associated with meeting the additional demand.

The hidden cost

EPA’s national marginal electricity emissions factor estimates the emissions from generators that tend to respond when electricity demand changes. At the national level, EPA’s factor is about 0.67 kilograms of CO₂ per additional kilowatt-hour, though the actual number varies considerably by region and time [4].

Using that factor and the baseline increase from equation (i):

\[ 0.572\,\text{kWh}\times0.672\,\frac{\text{kg CO}_2}{\text{kWh}} =0.383\,\text{kg CO}_2 \]

So, that one-degree adjustment is associated with roughly 383 grams of additional CO₂ per household per summer day. For one household, it isn’t alarming. But now consider this change across thousands of households.

Show how electricity demand and emissions are connected

Electricity is an unusual product. The supply reaching your house may come from natural gas, coal, nuclear power, wind, solar, or hydroelectric generation, which do not all have the same emissions. So it would be misleading to simply say, “an extra kilowatt-hour emitted X kilograms of CO₂.” Instead, I’m asking a slightly different question: “What happens when electricity demand increases?”

At every moment, grid operators have to keep electricity generation and demand balanced. Some generators produce relatively steady amounts of power. Others increase or decrease their output as demand changes. The generators responding to that change are operating at the margin (responding to changes in demand).

One choice, multiplied

Imagine a midsize American suburb with, say, 50,000 households. Each home lowers its thermostat by one degree from the recommended 78°F and retains that setting throughout the 92 peak days of summer:

\[ 50{,}000\times0.572\,\frac{\text{kWh}}{\text{day}} \times0.672\,\frac{\text{kg CO}_2}{\text{kWh}} \times92\,\text{days} \approx1.77\text{ million kg CO}_2 \]

The result is roughly 1,770 metric tons of additional carbon emissions, from one hypothetical degree of additional cooling, across one midsize community over one summer.

Using EPA’s vehicle-emissions estimates, that number is comparable to driving a typical gasoline car about 4.5 million miles—roughly 180 times around the Earth—or to the annual greenhouse-gas emissions of about 410 gasoline-powered cars.

Show how regional electricity emissions differ

The same additional kWh does not have the same emissions consequence everywhere.

EPA’s 2022 eGRID marginal rate for the ERCOT region covering most of Texas, for example, was about 1,195 lb CO₂/MWh, compared with a U.S. weighted marginal rate of about 1,405 lb/MWh. Other regions are higher or lower.

A more geographically precise version of this model could therefore use the local grid’s emissions factor rather than the national one.

The loop

In 2022, the U.S. alone emitted about 6.34 billion metric tons of greenhouse gases (CO₂-equivalent), which amounts to an average of roughly 17 million metric tons per day [5]. Our hypothetical suburb’s 1,770 tons is tiny beside that number, which is precisely the point. Climate change is not built from one change in thermostat, one round-trip flight, or one factory. It emerges from accumulation at enormous scales across an energy system, and cooling has one of the important, uncomfortable relationships with that system.

Greenhouse gases trap additional heat in the atmosphere. As average temperatures rise and heat becomes more severe, people need more cooling. More cooling means greater electricity demand. Where that added electricity is supplied by emitting generators, it creates more greenhouse gases, which contribute to further warming. Consequently, we need more cooling.

The International Energy Agency has described increasing cooling demand as part of this destructive feedback loop: a warming climate creates greater demand for cooling, while emissions from the energy used for that cooling contribute to still more warming.

The lesson here is not that air conditioning is inherently bad. Amid rising temperatures, cooling can be a matter of health and survival for many vulnerable groups. The questioning here lies at the margin. There is a difference between keeping a home safe during extreme heat and cooling it several degrees further because that is what we have become accustomed to.

There is a difference between need and one more degree of convenience. That extra degree is precisely what I wanted to understand.

Where responsibility sits

One common argument in these discussions, I see, is that corporations and industries are the largest contributors to emissions and pollutants, so why should I, at the end of a heavy day’s work, be the one putting my AC on hold? Which is valid. However, I don’t think the logical conclusion is that because something else has a much larger footprint, mine becomes null. It is, rather, a negative-sum game where both actions actively worsen the climate.

Corporate accountability is critical, and so are individual actions multiplied by millions of households that create massive collective impact. This is what the numbers above are meant to illustrate. One household’s extra degree is almost insignificant, but the same insignificant choice across tens of thousands—or millions—of households stops being so invisible.

Those systems, together, matter enormously. Better building codes, more efficient air conditioners, cleaner energy generation, accountability, and policy all matter. And so do our choices.

Be informed, be aware, and understand your everyday impact.
